PART Ⅰ

Background — 125 Years of Strata

This war did not suddenly arise in 2026. Twelve strata have piled up, and in each stratum a safety pin has come loose. The reason this part is the longest in this document is that almost everything that happened in 2026 has its origin here.

Ⅰ-1 · 1901–1953

Oil and Sovereignty — Every Iran Crisis Has an Oil Clause

A single contract signed in 1901 created every conflict in modern Iranian history. The basic emotion Iranians feel when looking at foreign powers — "foreigners are taking what is ours" — began here.

The D'Arcy Concession and British Oil

  • In 1901, the Englishman William Knox D'Arcy obtained a 60-year oil concession over most of Persia. In exchange, he paid 20,000 pounds in cash, 20,000 pounds in shares, and 16% of future net profits.
  • On May 26, 1908, the Middle East's first commercial oil field was discovered at Masjed Soleyman. In 1909 the Anglo-Persian Oil Company (APOC) was established, and in 1914, First Lord of the Admiralty Churchill purchased a 51% stake on behalf of the British government. From that moment, Iran's oil became fuel for the British Navy.
  • The core dispute lay in the phrase "16% of net profits." Iran had no access to APOC's accounts, and the structure was such that the taxes the British government collected from APOC exceeded the royalties Iran received.

The Constitutional Revolution — Ancestor of Today's Guardian Council

1905 when two sugar merchants were flogged over a price dispute, it sparked the Constitutional Revolution, which produced Iran's first parliament (Majlis) and constitution in 1906. However, the 1907 October Supplementary Fundamental Law established Twelver Shia Islam as the state religion, and mandated that a committee of five clerics review whether all laws comply with sharia. This is where the direct ancestor of today's Guardian Council, which effectively controls Iranian politics, was created.

That same year, the Anglo-Russian Convention divided Iran into a northern (Russian) zone, a southern (British) zone, and a neutral zone without Iran's participation, and in 1908 a Cossack Brigade led by a Russian officer shelled the parliament. In 1911, a Russian ultimatum led to the dissolution of parliament, ending the revolution. It was at this point that the experience of "great powers dividing us over our heads" became engraved in memory.

Reza Shah — The Archetype of Modernization and Backlash

1921 coup brought the Cossack Brigade commander Reza Khan to prominence, and in 1925 he abolished the Qajar dynasty and opened the Pahlavi dynasty. What he did was modernization itself — the Trans-Iranian Railway (1927~38), the University of Tehran (1934), secular civil law and courts, conscription, and from 1935 having the country's name changed from 'Persia' to 'Iran'.

However, the 1936 January forced unveiling decree (Kashf-e Hijab) was enforced by police tearing off women's chadors in the streets, and the 1935 clerical protest at the Goharshad Mosque in Mashhad was suppressed with bloodshed. This is where the pendulum swing of modern Iranian history between secular modernization and religious backlash begins. Much of the emotional fuel for the 1979 revolution comes from memories of this period.

Reza Shah canceled the D'Arcy concession in 1932, but the new 1933 agreement, while slightly improving terms, extended the concession period to 1993, which was in fact received in Iran as a defeat. In 1941, Britain and the Soviet Union invaded Iran (over the issue of German technicians and to secure a supply route to the USSR), and he abdicated and died in South Africa.

Why this is decisive

Known in the Iranian calendar as '28 Mordad', this incident is the genesis of Iranian anti-Americanism. Before this, unlike Britain and Russia, the United States was seen by Iranians as a friendly third party without a history of imperialism. After 1953, the United States became the guarantor of dictatorship. The Islamic Republic still repeatedly cites this incident today, and the direct motive behind the 1979 seizure of the US embassy was also the fear that "the US would repeat 1953 again."

Ⅰ-2 · 1953–1979

The Pahlavi Regime and Revolution — Collapse Built by Oil

SAVAK

Founded in 1957 with CIA support, and joined by Mossad in training from 1960, this was the secret police. In scale, the organization itself claimed fewer than 2,000 members, while historians estimate 4,000 to 6,000 full-time staff backed by a vast network of informants (the postrevolutionary claim of 15,000 has not been verified). Electric shock torture, flogging, and beatings are documented, and in 1976 Amnesty International rated Iran's human rights record among the worst in the world. What SAVAK actually accomplished was annihilating the secular opposition — the National Front (liberal), the Tudeh Party (communist), and the Fedayeen and MEK (guerrilla groups) were all destroyed, and the mosque remained the only space the state could not fully control. This explains why the clergy took over the revolution in 1979.

The White Revolution — A Failure Born of Success

  • Reforms in six points (later expanded to nineteen) beginning in January of 1963: land reform, nationalization of forests, sale of state enterprises, worker profit-sharing, women's suffrage, and the Literacy Corps.
  • The achievements were real — land was redistributed to about 2.5 million households, nearly 90% of tenant farmers became landowners on paper (affecting roughly 40% of the population), and the literacy rate rose from 26% to 42%.
  • However, most of the distributed land was too small to be self-sufficient, and credit and irrigation support were lacking. The result was the destruction of rural social order and mass migration to the cities — the slums of southern Tehran expanded, and these people became the foot soldiers of the 1979 revolution.
  • For the clergy, this was a triple blow. Land reform eroded religious endowments (waqf), women's suffrage and non-Muslims entering public office were branded anti-Islamic, and secular courts stripped the clergy of their judicial role.
  • In June 1963, Khomeini attacked the Shah and Israel in an Ashura sermon, was arrested, and three days of riots broke out (deaths: government figure 90-200, Khomeini's claim 15,000 — an unreliable figure). After denouncing the 1964 capitulation law granting extraterritoriality to US troops, he was exiled and stayed in Najaf, Iraq for 13 years from 1965.

The 1973 Oil Boom — How Money Brought Down the Regime

Due to the 1973 oil shock, Iran's oil revenue grew from about 1972 approximately $2.4 billion to 1974~75 $17.8 billion, roughly a 7-fold increase in three years. The Shah refused phased implementation and poured it all in. The result was a textbook case of Dutch disease.

  • Import bottlenecks — ships waited for months at Khorramshahr port while cargo rotted
  • Inflation of 15–25% from 1975–77, along with a real estate and construction bubble
  • An explosion in military spending — Iran became the single largest purchaser of US weapons (over $10 billion in orders between 1972–77)
  • Rural collapse and rising food imports, alongside visibly extreme inequality
  • When oil prices fell, an anti-profiteering crackdown led to the arrest of thousands of merchants, turning even the bazaar, the regime's last non-state social base, against it

On top of this came symbolic missteps. The 1971 2,500th anniversary celebration of the Persian Empire at Persepolis was an event that flew in food from Maxim's in Paris and mobilized 250 Mercedes 600s (costs vary wildly, from the government-announced $17 million to the BBC's claim of $120 million). Khomeini, from Najaf, called it "the devil's festival." In 1976, the abolition of the Islamic calendar and introduction of an imperial calendar dated from Cyrus the Great (making 1355 into 2535) enraged the faithful. In 1975, political parties were abolished and a single Rastakhiz Party was created, with the ultimatum "join or leave the country" — effectively closing off every legitimate channel for expressing grievances.

Death tolls range from 86–89 according to the government to thousands according to the opposition (an extreme discrepancy). Regardless of the actual number, the possibility of compromise ended on this day.

1978.10.06

Khomeini, expelled from Iraq at the Shah's request, arrives in Neauphle-le-Château near Paris. Paradoxically, gaining access to the international press and a direct phone line to Tehran causes his influence to surge.

1978.10–12

The general strike of oil workers brings exports to zero by December — a fatal blow to state finances.

1978.12.10–11

Regarding revolution deaths, the Islamic Republic officially claims 60,000 martyrs, but academic research estimates 2,000 to 3,000 across the entire 1978–79 period. It is one of the largest gaps between official figures and academic estimates in modern history.

Velayat-e Faqih — The Hidden Agenda

The 'Guardianship of the Jurist', formalized by Khomeini in his 1970 Najaf lectures, was a break from over 1,000 years of mainstream Shia quietism. The mainstream view held that all governments during the Occultation (Ghaybah) of the 12th Imam lacked legitimacy, and that clerics should guide rather than rule. Even Khomeini himself wrote in a 1943 work, "I do not say that government must be in the hands of the jurist."

What matters is that he concealed this agenda throughout the revolution. In Paris, he told Western journalists that clergy would not hold public office and that he himself would return to Qom. And nearly all Grand Ayatollahs rejected Velayat-e Faqih — Khoei of Najaf, Shariatmadari of Qom (who was stripped of his marja status in 1982 and subjected to the unprecedented punishment of house arrest), Golpaygani, and later Montazeri. Sistani of Najaf still maintains quietism today. In other words, Iran's system is an exception that mainstream Shia Islam has never endorsed.

Takeover of the Revolution and the Hostage Crisis

  • Elimination of the liberals: The provisional Bazargan government had no control over the Revolutionary Committees, the Revolutionary Courts (led by the 'Hanging Judge' Khalkhali, who executed hundreds in 1979 alone), or the newly created IRGC (founded May 5, 1979), and it resigned two days after the embassy takeover.
  • Elimination of the left: The Cultural Revolution of April 1980 shut down universities for about three years and purged tens of thousands of professors and students.
  • Elimination of the nationalists: The first president, Bani-Sadr (who won 75.6% of the vote in 1980), was impeached in June 1981 and fled into exile in Paris.
  • Terror and counter-terror of 1981–82: A bomb by the MEK at IRP headquarters killed Chief Justice Beheshti and about 70 others, and in August, the president and prime minister were killed simultaneously in a bombing. The state's retaliation was overwhelming — Amnesty International recorded 2,946 executions in 1981 alone. Ali Khamenei became president after his right arm was permanently paralyzed in an assassination attempt in 1981.
  • 1988 prison massacre: Immediately after the MEK's invasion operation, a fatwa from Khomeini led to the mass execution of prisoners. Tehran's 'Death Committee' included Ebrahim Raisi, who would later become president (the basis for his nickname "the Butcher of Tehran"). Death toll estimates: Montazeri's memoir says 2,800 to 3,800; HRW and Amnesty say 2,800 to 5,000; the MEK claims 30,000 (unverified). Montazeri, who criticized this, was removed from his position as successor in March 1989, creating a succession vacuum when Khomeini died three months later.

The hostage crisis began on November 4, 1979, and ended after 444 days with 52 hostages, minutes after Reagan's inauguration on January 20, 1981. The trigger was the U.S. allowing the Shah to enter the country for cancer treatment, which Iranians read as a prelude to "another 1953." Khomeini retroactively endorsed it, calling it "the Second Revolution, greater than the First," and indeed used it as leverage to purge the liberals and pass the December constitution. In April 1980, the U.S. severed diplomatic relations — with Switzerland representing U.S. interests and Pakistan representing Iranian interests, a structure that has remained unchanged for 46 years.

Ⅰ-3 · The System

The Machine Called the Islamic Republic — How It Works, and Why It Stopped

Iran has a dual structure of elected and unelected bodies. For every elected body, an unelected body screens candidates before the vote, and another unelected body can overturn the results after the vote. Understanding this structure resolves the question, "Iran has elections, so why does nothing change?"

Powers of the Supreme Leader (Constitution Article 110)

  1. Determining the general policies of the system and supervising their implementation
  2. The power to call a referendum
  3. Supreme command of the entire military, declaration of war and peace, and mobilization orders
  4. Appointment authority — 6 clerics of the Guardian Council, head of the Judiciary, head of the state broadcaster (IRIB), IRGC Commander-in-Chief, Chief of the regular army, Chief of the Joint Staff, commanders of each branch of the armed forces
  5. Mediating disputes among the three branches of government, resolving deadlocks through the Expediency Council
  6. Confirmation of the President's election (Tanfiz) — the President only takes office once the Supreme Leader signs off
  7. Authority to dismiss the President (after a Supreme Court ruling or a parliamentary vote of no confidence)
  8. Pardons and commutations of sentences

The real power beyond the constitution is even greater — appointing Friday prayer leaders in cities across the country (a nationwide political network), control of the Bonyads, and representatives of the Supreme Leader stationed in every ministry, university, IRGC unit, and provincial government. It is estimated that there are about 2,000 directly appointed positions across the state and quasi-state sectors.

The 1989 Succession — The Key to Understanding 2026

The most important precedent in this document

When Khomeini died in June 1989, Ali Khamenei was not a marja (source of emulation) but a Hojatoleslam, one rank lower. At the time, the constitution required marja status for the Supreme Leader. Nevertheless, coordinated by Rafsanjani, the Assembly of Experts elected him as an 'interim' leader (60 out of 74 votes), and then on July 28 of that same year, a constitutional referendum removed the marja requirement itself, which was reconfirmed in August. In 1994, the faculty of the Qom seminary 'declared' him a marja, but many grand ayatollahs, including Montazeri and Sistani, never recognized him as such.

The principle established here: the Supreme Leader's qualifications are not a binding constraint, but an obstacle that the ruling coalition can remove in order to install the candidate it wants. The fact that in March 2026 Mojtaba Khamenei could be elected through an online vote with even lower religious credentials than his father is because the requirement had already been removed back in 1989. A maneuver from 37 years ago made today's hereditary succession possible.

Five Doctrines Forged by the Eight-Year War

Every means Iran used in 2026 — missiles, proxies, self-sufficient production, martyrdom mobilization, distrust of international institutions — was all forged in this war. This chapter carries the heaviest weight in Part Ⅰ.

Causes and Course

The immediate point of contention was the Shatt al-Arab waterway. Behind this lay fear of Khomeini's appeal to Iraq's Shia majority to export the revolution, and Saddam's ambition to become the leader of the Arab world in place of Egypt, which had been isolated after Camp David.

Urban Warfare — Where Missile Doctrine Was Born

In the 'War of the Cities' waged over five rounds from 1984–88, Iraq directly struck Iranian cities with Scud and improved Al-Hussein missiles. In the final campaign of January–February 1988, Iraq launched 533 ballistic missiles, hitting 414 of them, and 2,312 civilians were killed. Tehran alone was hit by 118 missiles over 52 days, and about 1 million people fled the city every night.

Iran had no means of retaliation whatsoever. This sense of helplessness is the founding experience behind what is today the largest ballistic missile force in the Middle East. The missiles that flew into Israeli cities in 2024 and 2026 are the doctrinal descendants of the Al-Hussein missiles that fell on Tehran in 1988.

Chemical Weapons and the Silence of the International Community

  • From October 1983, Iraq systematically used mustard gas, tabun, and sarin, expending more than 100,000 chemical shells. More than 100,000 Iranians were victims of chemical weapons, and even decades later more than 70,000 are still receiving treatment.
  • In June 1987, mustard gas was dropped on Sardasht, a city of 12,000 people, killing 130 and wounding 8,000. Iran calls this the first use of chemical weapons on a city since Hiroshima. In March 1988 at Halabja, 3,200–5,000 Kurdish civilians were killed.
  • What is decisive is the international community's response. UN Security Council statements in 1984–85 did not name Iraq, and Resolution 582 in 1986 condemned the use [of chemical weapons] without identifying the perpetrator. Not a single resolution between 1986 and 1988 named Iraq. Furthermore, declassified documents show that the United States continued providing targeting intelligence to Iraq even while knowing it was using chemical weapons.

The Tanker War and the Vincennes

In the Tanker War, which began in 1981 and intensified in 1984, according to Lloyd's tallies 546 merchant ships were damaged and about 430 civilian sailors were killed (283 attacks by Iraq, 168 by Iran). The United States intervened with Operation Earnest Will (July 1987–September 1988, the largest convoy operation since World War II), reflagging Kuwaiti tankers under the American flag and escorting them, and with Operation Praying Mantis (April 18, 1988, the largest surface engagement by the US Navy since World War II), which struck the Iranian navy.

Then on July 3, 1988, the cruiser USS Vincennes shot down Iran Air Flight 655, a scheduled flight from Bandar Abbas to Dubai, killing all 290 people aboard (including 66 children). The United States did not issue a formal apology and settled in 1996 for $131.8 million. The captain received a commendation medal that made no mention of the shootdown. Tehran interpreted this as "a signal that the US was entering the war", and two weeks later Khomeini accepted the ceasefire resolution.

Upon accepting UN Resolution 598 on July 17, 1988, Khomeini's words are still quoted today — "Making this decision is more deadly than drinking poison. (…) Happy are those who departed through martyrdom. Unhappy am I that I have remained alive." The 8-year war ended with no change in territory and no reparations, reverting to the terms of the 1975 Algiers Accord.

Total Losses, and the Five Doctrines That Remained

Total deaths are estimated at about 500,000 (Iran's official war dead numbering 123,220–160,000, with 320,000–500,000 wounded), and the economic cost was about $627 billion for Iran and about $561 billion for Iraq. The Gulf states lent Iraq $37–60 billion — another reason Iran does not trust the Arab Gulf states.

Five Doctrines Carrying Through to 2026
  1. Missiles as a substitute for air power. The experience of having no means to counter the Al-Hussein → the region's largest missile force
  2. Self-sufficient production (Khodkafa'i). The experience of fighting under a total arms embargo → domestic arms production, and later domestic enrichment
  3. Martyrdom and forward defense. The institutionalization of the Basij mobilization culture, and the logic of "fighting on someone else's land" → the proxy network
  4. Structural distrust of international institutions. The fact that the Security Council did not even name the country that used chemical weapons is the basis of Iran's standard response today to demands from the IAEA and Security Council — "International law has never protected us." This sentiment also underlies Iran's legal denial of the 2025 snapback sanctions
  5. Deterrence must be built by oneself. The experience that no outside guarantor ever intervened → enrichment as insurance, not as an energy source

Ⅰ-5 · 1948–2025

Israel–Iran — A Nation That Went From Ally to Enemy

Hard to imagine now, but Iran was once Israel's largest crude oil supplier and an intelligence ally. Understanding how this relationship flipped shows that the hostility of 2026 is not religious destiny but a product shaped by the Cold War and the regional order.

The Alliance of the Shah Era (1948–1979)

  • In 1948, Iran, following Turkey, became the second Muslim-majority country to de facto recognize Israel.
  • Ben-Gurion's 'alliance of the periphery' doctrine — a strategy of joining hands with non-Arab states surrounding the Arab world. The 1958 Trident Agreement institutionalized trilateral intelligence cooperation between Mossad, Turkey's MİT, and Iran's SAVAK, targeting Ba'athist Iraq and Syria. Mossad and SAVAK even jointly supported Iraq's Kurdish rebels (Barzani).
  • Oil: After the Suez was closed by the 1967 war, Iran became Israel's major crude oil supplier, with the jointly owned Eilat–Ashkelon Pipeline serving as the conduit.
  • From 1977 to 1979, a joint surface-to-surface missile development program was even carried out under the name 'Flower Project'.

The Break, Yet the Duality of the 1980s

In 1979, Khomeini defined Israel as the 'Little Satan' and handed over the building of the Israeli embassy in Tehran to the PLO. However, actual actions differed from the rhetoric. Israel sold weapons to Iran throughout the Iran-Iraq War — about $75 million worth in 1981's 'Operation Seashell', a total of about $500 million from 1981 to 1983, and around 100 Israeli technical advisors stationed in Iran.

The culmination was the Iran-Contra affair (1985–86). Using Israel as a conduit, the U.S. handed over TOW anti-tank missiles and Hawk air defense missiles to Iran, and used the proceeds to support the Nicaraguan Contra rebels. In May 1986, former National Security Advisor McFarlane secretly traveled to Tehran but failed, and in November of that year, a Lebanese magazine exposed the affair, shaking American politics. Israel's logic remained the periphery doctrine — the near enemy was Iraq, and Iran was leverage.

Why It Flipped in the 1990s — Four Factors

  1. Iraq's defeat in 1991 eliminated the common enemy the two countries had shared.
  2. With the collapse of the Soviet Union, Cold War logic disappeared, leaving Iran as the sole remaining revisionist power in the region.
  3. The 1993 Oslo Accords — Israel needed a new threat narrative to keep U.S. strategic attention, and Iran, positioning itself as the leader of the anti-Oslo rejectionist camp, began supporting Hamas and Islamic Jihad.
  4. The growth of Hezbollah and Iran's nuclear program turned the threat from rhetoric into reality.

In this transitional period, there were two bombing attacks in Buenos Aires — the 1992 attack on the Israeli Embassy (29 dead) and the 1994 attack on the AMIA Jewish Community Center (85 dead). In 2006, Argentine prosecutors officially indicted, stating that Iran ordered and Hezbollah carried out the attacks, and Interpol's 2007 red notice list included Ahmad Vahidi, who later became Iran's Defense Minister. He is the very same person who became IRGC Commander-in-Chief in August 2026.

Ⅰ-6 · 1982–2024

The Axis of Resistance — 40 Years to Build, 14 Months to Collapse

Iran's deterrence strategy was to create buffer zones outside its borders. How this network was built and why it collapsed in 2024 is a precondition for the 2026 invasion decision.

Hezbollah — The Most Successful Investment

  • In 1982, right after Israel's invasion of Lebanon, Iran dispatched about 1,500 IRGC instructors to the Bekaa Valley to establish it.
  • 1983: the April Beirut US Embassy bombing (63 dead, most of the CIA's Beirut station killed) and the October Marine barracks bombing (241 US troops and 58 French troops killed). Reagan ordered a withdrawal 4 months later — this is where the lesson was created that asymmetric attacks can make a superpower retreat.
  • Israel's withdrawal from South Lebanon in 2000 became Hezbollah's founding myth, and in the 34-day war of 2006 it survived while firing about 4,000 rockets.
  • Its intervention in the Syrian civil war in 2013 (the Battle of Qusayr) transformed it from a 'resistance organization' into a regional expeditionary force, while simultaneously losing support in the Sunni Arab world.
  • Its arsenal grew from about 14,000 rockets in 2006 to over 150,000 in 2023, and Iran's support was estimated at about $700 million per year (2018 US Treasury Department estimate).
  • And then collapse in 2024: on September 17–18, simultaneous explosions of pagers and walkie-talkies killed 42 and wounded 3,500–4,000 (about 1,500 combatants lost combat capability), Nasrallah's death on September 27 (after a 32-year tenure), a ground invasion in October, and a ceasefire on November 27. Iran's greatest asset, built over 40 years, was neutralized in just two months.

Iraq — The Country the US Handed Over to Iran

The US invasion of Iraq in 2003 was the single greatest transfer of power to Iran in modern history. This is because the Sunni Ba'athist state that had contained Iran disappeared, and Shia parties that had been exiled in Iran in the 1980s came to power. SCIRI was founded in Tehran in 1982, and its militia, the Badr Corps, fought on Iran's side in the Iran-Iraq War. The Dawa Party took refuge in Iran in the 1980s and produced two prime ministers, Maliki and Abadi.

Facility Map

  • Natanz — the main underground enrichment facility. As of June 2025, about 18,000 centrifuges are underground. The above-ground pilot facility (PFEP) carried out 20%·60% enrichment.

Three Technical Points for General Readers

Know just this, and the news becomes readable
  1. Why 60% is dangerous. Natural uranium's U-235 is 0.711%, and weapons-grade is 90% or higher. However, the separative work (SWU) is concentrated at the front end — reaching 20% from natural uranium consumes about 90% of the total work, and reaching 60% consumes 98~99%. In other words, a country holding a 60% stockpile is, from an energy standpoint, already nearly weapons-grade, and 60% enrichment has no civilian use whatsoever.
  2. Centrifuge generations. If the IR-1 produces 0.8~1 SWU per year, the IR-6 produces 6~10 times that. Even keeping the same number of units, simply changing the model collapses the breakout time. In early 2025, the newer models exceeded IR-1 output for the first time.
  3. Breakout time. This is the time required to produce material for one weapon (25kg of U-235 per IAEA standard), and it is an indicator of capability, not intent. Under the JCPOA it was about 12 months → in 2022~23 about 12 days → by late 2024, enough for 5~6 weapons within 2 weeks. As of 2026, calculation itself is impossible — because facilities have been destroyed and, with no inspections, no one knows where the stockpiles are located.

The Fatwa, and the NPT

Iran's official position rests on Khamenei's fatwa prohibiting nuclear weapons (verbally mentioned in 2003, formally cited to the IAEA in 2005) and the "inalienable right" under NPT Article 4. There are two counterarguments — that no certified original document of the fatwa has ever been produced, and that a fatwa is inherently subject to change depending on circumstances. Indeed, in February 2025 there were reports that IRGC commanders demanded the fatwa be rescinded, and in March, Larijani said, "If attacked, Iran will build nuclear weapons."

To state the situation as of August 2026 in one sentence: Iran remains a party to the NPT, but IAEA verification has been effectively suspended since June~July 2025, and completely halted since February 28, 2026. This is the worst kind of gray zone — legally a member state, but practically an unverified state.

What Sanctions Actually Created — the Rent of Multiple Exchange Rates

This is the core pathology of Iran's economy

In Iran, at least three and as many as eight exchange rates exist simultaneously. There is the official preferential rate of 42,000 rials (fixed in 2018, used for importing essential goods such as medicine, wheat, and meat), the NIMA rate at which exporting companies are required to sell foreign currency, and the market rate (about 1.9 million rials).

The gap between 42,000 and 1,900,000 is about 45 times. This difference is rent that goes entirely to those holding import licenses. In other words, sanctions have created suffering for the people while generating enormous wealth for the small number holding licenses. Unifying the exchange rate is recommended by every economist, but it is politically almost impossible because it would immediately cause consumer prices to skyrocket.

The same logic applies across the entire sanctions-evasion economy. Shadow fleet operators, currency exchange networks, and IRGC-affiliated brokers take the spread. In 2013, Babak Zanjani was arrested on charges of embezzling $2.7 billion in oil payments that should have been remitted. This is the strongest evidence for the claim that "sanctions did not weaken the hardline security forces but instead concentrated power in their hands."

Subsidies, and the Terror Left Behind by November 2019

Iran's energy subsidies are among the largest in the world, at roughly $52 billion a year for petroleum products and about $30 billion for electricity. Gasoline is so cheap that about 1.5 billion liters are smuggled annually. On November 15, 2019, when the government raised gasoline prices by 50-200% overnight, nationwide riots broke out within hours, and the government responded with a total internet blackout from November 16-23. Death tolls vary — 230 according to the Iranian government, at least 304 according to Amnesty, and about 1,500 according to Reuters. This is why no subsequent Iranian government has been able to touch fuel prices, and the same held true even amid the 2025-26 fiscal crisis.

Energy Shortages in a Resource-Rich Nation

Despite having the world's 2nd-largest gas reserves and 4th-largest crude oil reserves, Iran suffers from chronic energy shortages. Winter gas shortages of 250 million~300 million㎥ per day, a summer 2024 electricity shortfall of 14,000MW, and in early 2025, 80 out of 600 power plants shut down. The causes are waste created by subsidies, thermal power generation efficiency of just 33%, transmission losses of 13%, gas flaring of 18 billion㎥ annually, and lack of investment. The result was nationwide school and business closures from December 2024 to February 2025, with about 50% of industry shut down. Combined with a water crisis in which 80% of wetlands have dried up, the World Bank now cites water and energy shortages alongside sanctions as factors hindering growth.

The Relationships Iran Has Leaned On

  • China — An indispensable partner. It purchases 80~93% of Iran's crude oil, and in March 2021 the two signed a 25-Year Comprehensive Cooperation Agreement. The widely-cited figure of "$400 billion" originated from a trade publication report and has never been confirmed by either government, and actual investment has come nowhere close to that amount.
  • Russia — A 20-Year Comprehensive Strategic Partnership Treaty in January 2025. However, it contains no mutual defense clause — it only commits to not aiding an aggressor, and in practice Russia did not help Iran in either 2025 or 2026. Trade amounts to only $4~5 billion per year.
  • UAE — For 40 years, it served as Iran's window for hard currency, re-export trade, and informal finance. About 30% of Iran's imports (worth $21 billion in 2024) and 90% of Gulf trade passed through it. The UAE's complete shutdown of this route on August 19, 2026 accomplished in a single stroke what 40 years of U.S. sanctions had failed to do.
  • Joining the SCO (2023) and BRICS (2024) — There has been no substantive sanctions relief. The decisive constraint is the risk of dollar exposure for private banks, and membership in any multilateral organization does not resolve that.

I-9 · Society

Those Who Have Already Left — Population, Women, Brain Drain, and Ethnic Minorities

The most frequently overlooked aspect when examining Iranian politics is social indicators. This country's population has already moved in a different direction from the regime.

Population — It Exploded, Then Suddenly Stopped

  • About 33.7 million in 1976 → about 37 million in 1979 → about 92 million in 2025. The population more than doubled during the first 40 years of the Islamic Republic.
  • The state-encouraged post-revolution baby boom (1979~88) during wartime pushed the annual population growth rate to 3.9~4.0% — one of the highest rates in human history. This generation became the 'youth bulge' that supported Khatami in 1997, drove the protests of 1999 and 2009, and caused the explosion in demand for university education.
  • And then followed one of the sharpest fertility collapses in modern history. The total fertility rate fell from 6.5~7.0 in the early 1980s to 2.0 in 2000, and to 1.6~1.7 in 2021 (currently estimated at 1.4~1.5). The state family planning program from 1989~2006 was a globally recognized success story, but in 2012 the Supreme Leader shifted course toward pronatalism, and the 2021 'Youthful Population Law' restricted contraception and abortion — yet the fertility rate did not budge.
  • The growth rate is now 0.7~1.2% annually, the median age is 32~33, and by the 2040s the country will become an aged society. There are no funds for pensions. The youth bulge has already passed, which means the pattern of future protests will be different.

Brain Drain

Iranians abroad are estimated at 4 to 5 million, with more than 30% in the United States. About 60% of Iranian immigrants in the US hold a bachelor's degree or higher, and more than 30% hold a master's or doctorate, making them one of the most highly educated immigrant groups in America. In 2024, the Minister of Higher Education admitted that about 25% of university professors had been lost to emigration. The cost estimate often cited is $50 billion a year (World Bank), but this is a rough and contested estimate — for reference, Iran's oil exports in 2019 were under $20 billion.

Ethnicity and Sect — Repression Concentrated on the Sunni Periphery

Since Iran's census does not ask about ethnicity, all figures are estimates. Roughly Persian 61-65%, Azerbaijani Turk 14-16%, Kurdish 7-10%, Lur 6%, Arab 2-3%, Baloch 2%. By sect, Shia 90-94%, Sunni 5-9%.

  • Azeris are fully integrated at the elite level — both Khamenei and his son, Mousavi, and Pezeshkian are all of Azeri descent. Their grievances are linguistic and cultural, not separatist.
  • The places actually suffering severe repression are the Sunni periphery — Kurds (very high execution rates, presence of armed groups such as Komala, PJAK, KDPI), Baloch (Sistan-Baluchestan is Iran's poorest province, and more than 90 people were killed on 'Bloody Friday' in Zahedan on September 30, 2022), Ahwazi Arabs (Khuzestan — producing most of Iran's oil while suffering water crises and pollution). Nearly half of the 2022 protest deaths came from Kurdistan and Sistan-Baluchestan.
  • The Baha'i (about 300,000) are not recognized under any provision of the constitution, are barred from university admission and public office, and are subject to property confiscation and imprisonment. By contrast, Jews (about 9,000), the largest Jewish community in the Muslim Middle East, hold one reserved seat in parliament.

Qom and Najaf — The Shia World Never Endorsed the Iranian System

Qom was rebuilt in 1922 by Haeri Yazdi, and until 1961 it was home to Borujerdi, the sole supreme marja of the Shia world at the time. His quietism kept Khomeini in check, and only after his death did Khomeini step to the forefront. Today Qom has more than 40,000 seminary students, but since 1980 it has been placed under state oversight through the seminary management council, and its finances also flow through the state and the bonyads — the point where its independence has been compromised.

Najaf is the older and more authoritative center dating from the 11th century, and Sistani (born 1930) is the marja with the largest following in the Shia world. He is a quietist, holding that clergy should advise and safeguard but not rule. Like his call for direct elections in 2004 and his anti-ISIS fatwa in 2014, this is a model in which he intervenes decisively only in existential crises and holds no permanent office.

Structural implication: Najaf is the living counter-model to Velayat-e Faqih, and the mainstream of Shia jurisprudence has never accepted Iran's system. The succession question for Sistani, now in his mid-90s, is another massive succession issue for the Shia world.

Hormuz — The Chokepoint by the Numbers

  • The narrowest point varies by source, ranging from 21 to 29 nautical miles, but the actual shipping lane is only 2 nautical miles in each direction, and lies within the territorial waters of Iran and Oman.
  • Transit volume (EIA): 21.9 million barrels per day in 2022 → 20.7 million in 2024 → 20.9 million in the first half of 2025 → 4.9 million in Q2 2026.
  • About 20% of world oil consumption, about 25% of seaborne crude trade, and more than 20% of global LNG trade. 93% of Qatar's LNG and 96% of the UAE's LNG pass through here, and there is no bypass route for LNG.
  • By destination (Q1 2025 crude): China 37.7%, India 14.7%, South Korea 12.0%, Japan 10.9% — these four account for about 75%. Asia as a whole accounts for 89.2%, while the US is 2.5% and Europe 3.8%. This is the structural reason the interests of the US and Asia diverge.
  • Virtually the only bypass pipelines are Saudi Arabia's East-West Pipeline (spare capacity of 3–5 million barrels) and the UAE's ADCOP (about 1.5 million), and together they cover less than 30% of normal transit volume. Iraq (south), Kuwait, Qatar, and Bahrain have essentially no alternative at all.
  • Legally, Iran is not a ratifying party to UNCLOS, and has maintained the position that it recognizes only the more restrictive innocent passage rather than the right of transit passage through international straits, claiming that it requires prior consultation for foreign warships.

The Conventional Wisdom That "Full Closure Is Self-Harm" — And the Third Path Iran Found

Until 2025, the standard expert view was as follows. ① China buys about 90% of Iranian crude, so a blockade would hit that sole customer ② Iran's own exports also depend on Hormuz (the Jask bypass line is essentially non-operational) ③ it would give Gulf Arab states a pretext to enter the conflict ④ precedent of the 5th Fleet and 1988's Praying Mantis ⑤ in Secretary of State Rubio's words, "economic suicide".

What Iran did in 2026 was bypass this conventional wisdom. Instead of fully closing the strait, it created a selective passage + toll system (PGSA), letting ships from friendly countries like China, Russia, and India pass while charging others $1–2 million per vessel. It turned the blockade from self-harm into a source of income and a bargaining chip, and this is the technical reason this war has not ended after six months.

Reference — Past Closure Threats

The British Navy's blockade of Abadan in 1951, the Tanker War of 1980–88(even then, over 98% of transiting vessels went unmolested and the strait was never actually closed), the threat over the EU embargo in 2011–12("easier than drinking water"), Rouhani's warnings and tanker attacks in 2018–19, Abqaiq in 2019, and in June 2025, the Iranian parliament's passage of a closure resolution(the final decision rests with the SNSC, so it was not implemented). A card that had only ever been threatened for decades was actually used for the first time in 2026.

International Institutions of Energy Security

Since its founding in 1974, the IEA has invoked collective action six times — the 1991 Gulf War, 2005 Katrina, 2011 Libya, 2022 Ukraine(twice), and the largest-ever release of 400 million barrels on March 11, 2026. National strategic reserves (as of end-2025) stand at approximately 1.4 billion barrels for China, 413 million for the US SPR, 263 million for Japan's government reserve, and 79 million for South Korea.

Ⅰ-11 · 1962–2023

Korea and the Middle East/Iran — 60 Years of Entanglement

Korea has become deeply entangled with this region three times — recouping oil money through the construction boom of the 1970s, paying the price of sanctions in the 2010s, and now receiving the bill again in 2026.

The 1970s — Recouping the Oil Shock through Construction Exports

  • It began in June 1973 when Samhwan Corporation won the contract for the Khaibar–Al-Ula highway (175km) in Saudi Arabia. A famous story tells of how, to shorten the construction period during the pilgrimage season, thousands of torches were lit in the desert for night work, and when this reached King Faisal, it is said to have led to an additional $65 million contract.
  • In February 1976, Hyundai Construction's contract for Saudi Arabia's Jubail Industrial Port was worth $930 million, equivalent to about a quarter of the South Korean government's annual budget at the time. Approximately 2.5 million person-years of labor were deployed.
  • Overseas construction contract amounts exploded from a cumulative $423 million during 1965–73 to $34.66 billion during 1974–81, of which 94% was in the Middle East. During the same period, overseas construction contract value came close to half of total exports, and the number of dispatched workers exceeded 100,000 in 1979 and more than 150,000 annually during 1981–83. In 1981, Korea ranked 2nd in the world in overseas construction contracts.
  • Significance: Recouping money accumulated in oil-producing countries through construction exports and thereby offsetting the burden of oil prices and the current account deficit was the key channel through which Korea overcame the first oil shock relatively quickly.

Nevertheless, the shock was severe. Due to the first oil shock, the growth rate fell from 14.8% in 1973 to around 7% in 1975, and consumer prices rose 24.7% in 1975. The second shock was even worse, resulting in negative growth in 1980 (−1.6% under the revised national accounts, −5.2% under the old accounts, figures vary by source), wholesale prices +38.9%, and a current account deficit of $5.3 billion, with domestic oil prices raised 7 times, a cumulative 337%, from March 1979 to November 1981.

The 2020 Cheonghae Unit Precedent — The Root of the Current Debate

쟁점

Was Iran really close to nuclear weapons

This is the casus belli of the war, and at the same time it is the most heavily disputed claim. Notably, much of the rebuttal came from within the U.S. government itself.

Internal contradiction in the logic

The Trump administration's two claims contradict each other. ① The June 2025 operation "completely destroyed" Iran's nuclear program. ② In February 2026, Iran's nuclear armament was imminent, making a preemptive strike unavoidable. Both cannot be true. This contradiction is a key reason why support for the war collapsed in US public opinion and Congress, and it also serves as the legal basis for Iran's demand for war reparations.

PART Ⅲ

Six Months of War — Detailed Course

The war passed through four phases. ① Decapitation and scorched-earth in February–March, ② regional spread in March, ③ containment and failed negotiations in April–June, ④ renewed escalation and stalemate in July–August.

Phase 1 · Late February – Early March

The Decapitation Operation and the First 72 Hours

2.28

Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei killed. Israeli aircraft dropped about 30 precision-guided munitions and 'Black Sparrow' air-launched ballistic missiles on a residential-bunker complex in Tehran. The target intelligence is reported to have been accumulated by the CIA over several months. His daughter, granddaughter, son-in-law, and daughter-in-law also died. Iran denied it for a day before confirming on March 1 and declaring 40 days of mourning.

2.28

The largest sortie in the history of the Israeli Air Force — about 200 fighter jets dropped over 1,200 munitions on 500 targets within 24 hours. The US military struck ballistic missile infrastructure with cruise missiles and B-1, B-2, and B-52 bombers.

2.28

A cyberattack cut Iran's internet down to 1–4% of normal levels for over 60 hours. A prayer-time app (BadeSaba) with 5 million downloads was hacked and used to send messages urging defection.

2.28

Iran's first counterattack, True Promise IV. Israeli estimates put about 170 ballistic missiles on the first day alone. Alongside Tel Aviv, Haifa, and Beersheba, US military bases across the Gulf were struck, including Al Udeid in Qatar, Ali Al Salem in Kuwait, Al Dhafra in the UAE, and the Fifth Fleet headquarters in Bahrain.

3.01

In Beit Shemesh, a missile penetrated air defenses and struck a synagogue and shelter directly, killing 9 and wounding 65. Amnesty International raised the possibility of violations of international humanitarian law.

2.28

A girls' school in Minab, southern Iran, was hit. Iran claims 180 deaths (some reports say 80–160) — a figure that exists only through Iranian government statements, but the International Commission of Jurists (ICJ) cited this incident in pointing out a violation of the UN Charter.

3.01

Formation of the Interim Leadership Council (Article 111 of the Constitution): Guardian Council member Arafi, Judiciary Chief Mohseni Eje'i, and President Pezeshkian.

3.02

After being struck, the US embassy in Kuwait was closed indefinitely. The consulate general in Dubai was hit by a drone, and the embassy in Riyadh was hit by a drone and closed. RAF Akrotiri, the British air base in Cyprus, was also attacked by drone. Qatar shot down two Iranian Su-24s — the first manned aircraft shootdown of the war.

3.02

A friendly-fire incident by a Kuwaiti F/A-18 shot down 3 US F-15Es (all 6 crew members ejected safely). Iran claimed the kills as its own, but the US military corrected this, attributing it to friendly fire.

Results of the Decapitation Operation — The Regime Did Not Collapse

The goals of the US and Israel effectively included regime change, and even the Supreme Leader was eliminated, but the regime did not collapse. Rather, in the face of foreign invasion, the momentum of protests was temporarily absorbed into unity, and succession was completed within ten days. This will long remain a topic of debate as an empirical case regarding the effectiveness of decapitation strategy.

Phase 2 · March

Expansion — Succession, Lebanon, the Sea, Energy

Succession and the War to Eliminate Leadership

Early March

The Assembly of Experts (88 members) elected Mojtaba Khamenei as the new Supreme Leader (reports differ on the election date, giving either March 3 or March 9). After five failed in-person meetings, the vote proceeded online, and after votes were cast but before the count was finished, the Assembly of Experts' bear office was airstruck. 8 members declared a boycott citing "pressure from the Revolutionary Guard."

3.01–04.06

A chain of eliminations of Iranian leaders: Mohammad Pakpour (IRGC Commander-in-Chief), Ali Shamkhani, Ali Larijani (3.17, while raising questions about the constitutionality of the online vote), Gholamreza Soleimani (Basij Commander), Intelligence Minister Esmail Khatib (3.18), Alireza Tangsiri (IRGC Navy Commander, in charge of the Hormuz blockade).

The Lebanese Front

3.02–03

Hezbollah resumed rocket attacks in retaliation for Khamenei's death. Israel designated this an "official declaration of war" and struck southern Beirut (Dahieh) and more than 70 locations in the south.

3.16

Israeli ground invasion. Ultimately 5 divisions (36th, 91st, 98th, 146th, 162nd) were deployed, and the occupied territory was a buffer zone 5–10km wide north of the Blue Line (called the 'Yellow Line' by the Israeli military); the UK House of Commons Library describes it by this width rather than area (the figure of 570–600㎢ presented by Wikipedia is not confirmed by an official source). On March 24, Defense Minister Katz declared he would "demolish border villages and permanently occupy up to the Litani River."

March–May

Battles at Bint Jbeil, Khiam (urban warfare and systematic demolition), and Hadatha. In mid-May the Israeli military crossed the Litani River, conducting operations for a week, and occupied the Beaufort Castle in late May.

Present

More than 4,175 deaths (UK House of Commons Library, as of June 22), and about 1.2 million displaced (18% of the population) (UN). Evacuation orders issued for approximately 2,000㎢ south of the Zahrani River. Infrastructure destroyed in more than 39 villages, some completely demolished.

The Sea — From the Gulf to the Indian Ocean

3.04

USS Charlotte (SSN-766) sinks the Iranian frigate IRIS Dena. 19–40 nautical miles south of Galle, Sri Lanka. The vessel was returning from a multinational exercise in India (MILAN), so it was not carrying ammunition. The death toll is reported as either 104 or 87 (sources vary). The first sinking by a submarine since the Falklands War, and the first by a US submarine since WWII. The single most legally contentious incident of the war.

3.06

The Iranian supply ship IRIS Bushehr, along with its crew of 208, was detained (internment) by the Sri Lankan Navy — the first internment of a warship by a neutral country since WWII.

2.28–March

The Iranian Navy was annihilated. Confirmed losses alone total 13 vessels (destroyers Jamaran, Sahand, Sabalan, the only operational submarine IRIS Fateh, drone carrier IRIS Shahid Bagheri, Kilo-class and Ghadir-class vessels, etc.). US Central Command claimed 130 vessels as of March 19, and later tallies claim 155 vessels sunk or damaged — the discrepancy stems from whether hundreds of small moored fast boats are included.

3.10–27

Iran began laying mines. On March 27, the IRGC officially declared the closure of the Strait of Hormuz (targeting vessels traveling to/from ports of the US, Israel, and allied nations). About 20,000 sailors and about 2,000 vessels were trapped inside the Gulf. Six cruise ships (15,000 passengers) were also stranded.

3.21

The US claimed Iran fired missiles toward Diego Garcia (a joint US-UK base in the Indian Ocean), with one destroyed in flight and one intercepted by an SM-3. Iran denied this, claiming it was an Israeli false-flag operation — no evidence that a missile actually approached has been made public.

The Energy War

3.02–08

Iran carried out a series of strikes on Gulf energy facilities, including Saudi Arabia's Aramco Ras Tanura (3.2, 3.4), the Shaybah oil field, Bahrain's BAPCO sole refinery (3.10 force majeure), Kuwait's KPC (3.7 force majeure), and the UAE's ADNOC Ruwais Refinery (3.11). On March 4, Iraq's national power grid was paralyzed.

3.06–08

Israel struck oil storage facilities near Tehran — a 'river of fire' and black acid rain. On March 14, more than 90 military targets on Kharg Island (accounting for 90% of Iran's crude oil exports) were struck, but the export terminal was deliberately left untouched.

3.18–19

The turning point of the energy war. Israel struck the world's largest gas field, South Pars → Iran destroyed Trains 4 and 6 of Qatar's Ras Laffan LNG complex. 17% (12.8 million tons annually) of Qatar's LNG export capacity was lost, and force majeure was declared to South Korea, China, Italy, and Belgium. The following day, Iran also struck Haifa's BAZAN Refinery.

3.21 · 3.27

The US dropped bunker busters on Natanz, and Iran retaliated by targeting the vicinity of Dimona (78 injured). On March 27, the Arak heavy water complex and the Ardakan yellowcake plant were damaged.

3.28

Yemen's Houthis officially entered the war. Ballistic missiles were fired at Israel. The Red Sea and Bab-el-Mandeb became the second danger zone.

3.31

China and Pakistan jointly propose a 5-point peace plan — immediate ceasefire, humanitarian access, restoration of normal transit through the Strait of Hormuz. The African Union expresses support.

Phase 3 · April–June

Blockade and Two Failed Agreements

4.05–07

Pakistan proposes a 45-day two-stage ceasefire plan; Iran rejects it and counter-proposes 10 points. Trump's ultimatum: "Tonight, an entire civilization will vanish and never return."

4.08

A 2-week ceasefire mediated by Pakistan is reached. Its core points: cessation of hostilities, reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, nuclear limitations, conditional easing of sanctions, and release of some frozen assets. China plays a decisive role in persuading Iran.

4.08

However, just hours later Israel strikes over 100 locations in Lebanon in 10 minutes in Operation 'Eternal Darkness', killing 357 people. Pakistan and Iran claim the ceasefire includes Lebanon, while the US and Israel claim it does not — the moment the agreement's flaw immediately became apparent.

4.11–12

Islamabad talks — 21 hours, 3 rounds (1 indirect, 2 direct). About 300 people on the US side including Vice President Vance, Witkoff, and Kushner, and about 70 on the Iranian side including Parliament Speaker Ghalibaf and Araghchi. Talks broke down over just two remaining issues (nuclear program and status of the Strait). Araghchi said "we were on the verge of signing the memorandum," while Vance explained "Iran had not abandoned its nuclear ambitions."

4.13

The US declares a naval blockade of Iranian ports (10 AM ET). Combined with Iran's blockade of the strait, this forms a 'dual blockade.' Iran calls it "an act of war and piracy." China called it "irresponsible and dangerous," Russia warned of an "economic earthquake," and the UK, France, Spain, Australia, and the EU refused to lend support.

4.16

A US-mediated Israel–Lebanon ceasefire (10 days, extendable). It is later extended by 3 weeks and 45 days, leading to a mutual understanding on June 1. Israeli forces remain stationed.

4.17–18

Iran declares the strait reopened → oil prices plunge 11% → since the US does not lift the blockade, it is reimposed within a day. This becomes the prototype of a pattern repeated for the next four months.

4.19–5.22

US Navy enforcement of the blockade: the Iranian cargo ship Touska has its engine room destroyed by 5-inch guns from USS Spruance and is seized (4.19); by May 22, 94 ships are intercepted and turned back. Iran retaliates by seizing the Greek ship Epaminondas and the MSC Francesca.

4.28–29

The UAE announces its withdrawal from OPEC and OPEC+ after 59 years (effective May 1). The largest-ever oil producer to leave, signaling a break with Saudi Arabia.

4.30

Brent crude hits an intraday high of $126 (a 4-year high). Dubai crude had already hit an all-time high of $166 on March 19.

5.04–06

The US launches escort operation Project Freedom to extract ships trapped in the Gulf → suspended within 48 hours, citing "significant progress."

5.25–29

Iran lifts its internet blackout (5.25). The US lifts the blockade (5.29).

6.07–09

Missile exchanges briefly resume.

6.14–17

The Islamabad Memorandum is signed. Representatives sign electronically on June 14; President Trump signs at the Palace of Versailles (during a G7 dinner) on June 17, while President Pezeshkian signs remotely from Tehran. A 60-day negotiation deadline is set. Full-scale war halts here.

Phase 4 · Late June–August

Collapse, Renewed Escalation, and Stalemate

6.18–24

The US lifts the blockade and Iran opens the strait, but as Iran imposes tolls exceeding $1 million per ship, transit is limited to only 4–15 ships a day. Trump insisted on "permanent free passage," while Iran countered it was a "service fee."

6.25–27

An Iranian drone attacks the merchant ship Ever Lovely → US retaliatory strike → Iran retaliates again.

7.07–08

The agreement collapses. Iran strikes three merchant ships in the strait (a fire on the Qatari LNG carrier Al Rekayat, the Saudi tanker Wedyan, etc.) → the US revokes Iran's crude oil export license → Trump declares the agreement "a waste of time" and resumes large-scale airstrikes. 80–90 targets in southern Iran are hit, destroying over 60 IRGC fast boats.

7.12–18

Iran again declares the strait closed. Over three days the US strikes 300 targets, and on July 16 conducts a 90-minute concentrated airstrike (Bandar Abbas, Semnan, Qeshm, Chabahar, etc.). Iran retaliates with Kheibar Shekan missiles against Kuwait, Bahrain, and Jordan's Azraq base. Over 8 airstrikes across 8 days, targets expand to include 6 bridges, power facilities, and even desalination facilities — Iran criticizes this as deliberate targeting of civilian infrastructure. More than 50 dead and over 500 wounded.

7.14

The US Navy resumes the blockade.

7.20

Houthis declare a naval blockade on ships calling at Saudi ports. Traffic through Bab el-Mandeb plunges from 41 ships a day to 29.

7.29

After Trump said "now it's our turn," CENTCOM declared the strikes "complete." From then until August 20, 21 consecutive days with no strikes on Iranian territory, and Iranian launches effectively at zero. The current lull begins.

8.05

Iran announces it has agreed with Oman on strait route coordinates. Inbound traffic uses the northern route through Iranian waters, outbound uses the southern route through Omani waters, with a structure of 60 days free initially followed by service fees.

8.10–14

Mojtaba reshuffles the security leadership — appointing Mohsen Rezaei as Secretary of the Supreme National Security Council and Ahmad Vahidi as IRGC Commander-in-Chief. Rezaei had openly opposed the June strait agreement. Iran subsequently adds ending the wars in Lebanon and Gaza as a condition for reopening the strait.

8.11–14

US forces disable the Panamanian-flagged container ship Vela Nova, which attempted to break the blockade, with a Hellfire missile. The Houthis attack the merchant vessel Tihamah in Bab el-Mandeb, killing 6 (including 2 rescuers). Three ADNOC-linked vessels are struck in succession within 48 hours, with the UAE condemning it as "piracy and economic coercion."

8.15

Southern Lebanon suffers its deadliest day since the June ceasefire (11 dead, 7 in Ansar). Hezbollah's promised retaliation has yet to be carried out.

8.16–17

The 60-day deadline expires. Negotiations never even began. Trump demands "unconditional surrender" from Iran and threatens to bomb Oman, the mediator. On the same day, he orders a major scale-back of the South Korea–US joint exercises, citing South Korea's refusal to support operations against Iran as one of the reasons.

8.19

Trump declares an 'Economic D-Day' — announcing the toughest sanctions ever, targeting oil smuggling, swap lines, cash couriers, and currency exchanges. On the same day, the UAE indefinitely suspends all trade and financial dealings with Iran. The impact is significant since Dubai is Iran's largest trading gateway, ahead of China and Turkey ($28 billion annually, roughly a third of Iran's imports).

What is the current lull?

The halt in strikes lasting over three weeks is not the result of an agreement. The US has shifted its pressure tactics from military strikes to economic blockade, while Iran conserves its missile stockpile and focuses on the strait as its final leverage. Analysts describe this lull as a 'load-bearing' state that suppresses escalation on the Lebanon, Iraq, and Yemen fronts. If it collapses, multiple fronts could open simultaneously.

PART Ⅳ

Military — What fought, and how

The military essence of this war comes down to one thing. There was complete asymmetry in conventional forces, and so Iran gave up fighting conventionally and instead took the sea hostage.

Forces

What each side deployed

United States

  • Carrier strike groups — USS Abraham Lincoln (arrived 1.26, deployed continuously for over 240 days, with crew mental health issues publicly raised), USS Gerald R. Ford, USS George H. W. Bush (additionally deployed 3.7). For rotation, USS George Washington passed through the Singapore Strait on August 13 — leaving no forward-deployed carrier in the Western Pacific.
  • Air power — B-1, B-2, B-52 bombers, F-22, F-35, F-15E, F-16, F/A-18, A-10, EA-18G electronic warfare aircraft, U-2, RC-135 reconnaissance aircraft, MQ-9. On the ground, Patriot, THAAD, HIMARS.
  • Bases — Al Udeid (Qatar), Ali Al Salem/Camp Buehring (Kuwait), Al Dhafra (UAE), the 5th Fleet in Bahrain, Prince Sultan (Saudi Arabia), Muwaffaq Salti/Azraq (Jordan), Erbil (Iraq), Ovda/Ben Gurion (Israel), Akrotiri (Cyprus), Fairford (UK), Ramstein (Germany), Diego Garcia, and in August, even Bezmer Air Base in Bulgaria.
  • At the start of the blockade, over 10,000 troops and more than 12 warships. By the July resumption, the standing scale was over 15,000 troops and more than 200 aircraft/vessels.

Israel

  • F-35I 'Adir', F-15 (operating the Rocks air-launched ballistic missile), Hermes 900/450, Heron, and Eitan drones.
  • 5 divisions in Lebanon (36th, 91st, 98th, 146th, 162nd). On the first day of the war, an additional 20,000 reservists were called up (on top of the existing 50,000).
  • Air defense consists of three layers: Arrow, David's Sling, and Iron Dome. Warning time is about 90 seconds, with roughly 10 minutes from detection to impact.

Iran

  • Dual structure — the regular Artesh (approx. 350,000) and the IRGC (approx. 190,000) coexist. The IRGC handles strategic missiles and the coastal navy, while the Artesh handles the ocean-going navy and conventional air force. The Basij militia numbers 90,000 on standing duty, and up to 1 million when mobilized.
  • Ballistic missiles — estimated at about 2,000 at the start of the war (reduced to 1,500 after the June 2025 war, then rebuilt). Shahab-3/Emad, Sejjil-2, Kheibar Shekan, Khorramshahr-4, Fateh-110/Zolfaghar. However, two-thirds of the launchers had already been destroyed in 2025.
  • Drones — the Shahed-136 is the mainstay, along with the Mohajer-6 and Ababil-3.
  • Air defense — Russian-made S-300PMU-2, domestically produced Bavar-373, Khordad-15, and Chinese-made HQ-9B around Tehran. As of March 14, about 90% was assessed as neutralized, and this assessment was not officially updated afterward.
  • Air force — about 250 combat-capable aircraft, but most are aging types such as the F-14, F-4, F-5, and MiG-29. They effectively contributed little to the war.

International Law

Three legal issues

1. The legality of the strike itself

The US cited Article 51 of the Charter (self-defense) (preemption against an imminent threat), while Israel cited an existential threat. The opposing side views this as a violation of Article 2(4) of the Charter, which prohibits the use of force, since there was no armed attack and no Security Council authorization. The International Commission of Jurists (ICJ, distinct from the International Court of Justice) issued a statement that the strike violated the Charter and international law, and the Secretary General of Amnesty International pointed to acts that could constitute war crimes.

2. Two blockades

  • Iran's blockade of the strait — the majority view holds this violates the right of transit passage under Part III of UNCLOS. While the 12-nautical-mile territorial sea is recognized, transit passage cannot be made subject to permission or fees. Mine-laying violates Hague Convention VIII. Strait states such as Singapore strongly oppose tolls because of the precedent it would set for the Strait of Malacca, Gibraltar, and the Danish straits.
  • The US blockade of the port — Iran, Russia, and China argue that blockading another country's port in peacetime is itself an act of war, and is unlawful without Security Council authorization or valid self-defense. The US failed to secure legal cover from the Security Council after its April 7 resolution was vetoed.

3. The third front: reparations lawsuits

Instead of filing with the International Court of Justice, Iran chose a strategy of domestic lawsuits, ICC referral, and universal jurisdiction. An organization under the judiciary mobilized about 19,000 experts and accumulated 50,000 expert opinions in Tehran alone, with the Minab school case compiled into 2,000 pages. More than 4 million Iranians submitted powers of attorney for a class-action damages lawsuit. Furthermore, Iran has tied countries that provided airspace, fuel, or landing rights into a 'chain of responsibility,' and seized four ships in the Gulf based on court enforcement orders.

This litigation campaign is not mere propaganda but the legal substance behind the demand for 'reparations,' making it an item that cannot be easily abandoned in negotiations.

PART Ⅵ

Politics — Four domestic politics are holding the war hostage

Half the reasons negotiations aren't succeeding aren't international. Domestic politics in Tehran, Washington, Jerusalem, and regional capitals are each making concessions impossible.

Iran

The regime survived, but it became a different regime

Hereditary succession and the legitimacy problem

Mojtaba Khamenei (born 1969) holds the rank of Hojatoleslam, and many point out that this falls short of the Ayatollah/Mujtahid qualifications required by the Velayat-e Faqih (Guardianship of the Jurist) system. The election process was also fragile.

  • After five failed in-person meetings, it proceeded via online voting — attendees testified to an "unnatural atmosphere" and abbreviated discussion
  • After the vote was cast but before the count, the Assembly of Experts' Qom office was struck by an airstrike (the Tehran building had already been bombed)
  • 8 members declared a boycott, citing "strong pressure from IRGC commanders"
  • Even the vote count itself is contested — the assembly announced it was "unanimous," but the New York Times reported 59 of 88 (with 29 votes for reform-leaning candidates), while Bloomberg and Iran International reported that only 50 to 59 ballots were collected
  • There is testimony that Khamenei's submitted will stated "he wanted an in-person election and opposed succession by his son or family" — this has not been independently verified
  • Ali Larijani, who had raised the unconstitutionality of the online vote and delayed the announcement, was eliminated by Israel on March 17

Reuel Gerecht and Ray Takeyh described this as "the collapse of the revolution's last egalitarian pillar." On rooftops, chants of "Death to Mojtaba" were heard.

Rumors of his resignation circulated in early June, but state media dismissed them as "baseless rumors," and this has not been independently confirmed.

  • The government declared the protests suppressed on January 21, but a second, university-centered phase began on February 21, spreading to Al-Zahra, Kurdistan, Sharif, Shahid Beheshti, and others. Supply Losses

  • Depending on the estimation method, 10 to 14 million barrels per day have vanished (World Bank and IMF: 10 million; Kpler: 11 million offline; ECB and Bruegel average: 14 million = 14% of global supply). For reference, the 2022 Ukraine shock was 1 million barrels.

  • Total bypass pipeline capacity is around 9 million barrels a day, which cannot replace the 20 million barrels that normally pass through the Strait of Hormuz. LNG has virtually no bypass route.
  • The United States helped fill part of the gap by hitting an all-time high of 13.93 million barrels per day in April.

OPEC's Paralysis and the UAE's Departure

OPEC+ decided on modest production increases in March, April, and July, but analysts assess that quotas have become "effectively meaningless" — because it is Hormuz, not allocation quotas, that determines actual supply. Amid this, the UAE announced on April 28–29 its withdrawal from OPEC and OPEC+ after 59 years (effective May 1). It is the largest oil-producing country ever to leave, interpreted as alignment with Washington and a break with Riyadh.

Strategic Petroleum Reserves — The Largest Release Ever

At the IEA's emergency board meeting on March 11, 32 member countries unanimously agreed to a joint release of 400 million barrels (actual pledge: 411.9 million). This is the largest release in IEA history, dwarfing the 180 million barrels released in 2022. The United States added 53.3 million barrels in May on top of its March release of 172 million, and Japan released 80 million barrels (15 days' worth). As a result, the U.S. SPR fell to 293.4 million barrels, its lowest level in 44 years since December 1982. China and India did not participate, as they are not IEA members.

Refining Margins and Retail Prices — The Most Painful Point Right Now

  • More than 20% of Middle Eastern refining capacity (9.6 million barrels per day) has been paralyzed, with regional refinery shutdowns reaching 2.2 million barrels per day as of August.
  • The U.S. diesel crack hit an all-time high of $102.20 per barrel on August 17, and the European diesel premium also hit an all-time high of about $95 on August 18.
  • U.S. retail prices (August 13): gasoline at $4.03 per gallon, diesel at $5.40. Up +$0.83 and +$1.70 year-on-year, respectively. Since August 12, gasoline has not fallen below $4 even once. The national average has already been above $4 for 103 days.
  • Due to Houthi attacks, Aramco's Jizan refinery has not shipped a single vessel of diesel since July 24; this facility had been responsible for 6–8% of EU and UK diesel imports since April.

Why Europe Hurt Less Than in 2022

According to ECB analysis, ① at the outbreak of war, crude oil was in a surplus of 2.5 million barrels per day (versus a shortage of 1 million barrels in 2022), ② the IEA released 400 million barrels, ③ Asia switched from gas to coal, reducing demand, and ④ the JKM–TTF spread turned positive, causing cargoes to flow to Asia and preventing a bidding war with Europe. While historical elasticity suggested an 81% rise in gas prices, the actual increase was 53%.

Iran's Toll System — Turning the Blockade into a Revenue Source

On May 18, Iran launched the 'Persian Gulf Strait Authority (PGSA)'. Shipowners submit ownership, insurance, crew manifest, cargo, and route by email, and only after PGSA approval and payment is made is a transit permit issued. The reported price is $2 million per voyage or $1 per barrel, and payment is made in yuan or cryptocurrency. Countries allowed to pass are selectively designated, including China, Russia, India, Iraq, Pakistan, Turkiye, Malaysia, Thailand, and the Philippines.

Here lies a legal trap. The U.S. Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) warned that paying transit fees to Iran-linked entities could constitute a prohibited transaction. In other words, even non-U.S. shipping companies, charterers, and insurers are exposed to secondary sanctions. Forbes described this as Hormuz turning into "a regime of fees and permits," and even President Trump remarked that the toll idea was "a beautiful thing and could change the rules of the world."

Central Banks — From Cuts to Hikes

  • The Fed (Chair Kevin Warsh) held rates steady for two consecutive meetings in June and July (3.50~3.75%), but three members dissented in favor of a hike at the July meeting, and the probability of a hike in September rose to 72%. The most symbolic macro fact of 2026 is that the oil shock flipped the Fed's next move from 'cut' to 'hike.'
  • The ECB carried out its first hike of this cycle on June 11 (25bp, 2.25%). With inflation at 3.2% and weak growth, this is a classic stagflation dilemma.

Markets — It's Bonds, Not Gold, That Are Talking

  • Stocks were surprisingly resilient. The Nasdaq entered a correction on March 26, but the AI capex cycle offset the geopolitical shock, with the energy sector being the biggest beneficiary.
  • Bonds are the real signal. On August 17, the U.S. 30-year Treasury yield hit 5.31%, the highest since June 2007. In March, stocks and bonds fell together, which signals not a growth shock but an inflation shock.
  • Gold was not the war's winner. The all-time high of $5,608 came in January, before the war, and in March it actually plunged sharply due to margin calls and a spike in real rates. On August 21 it stood at $4,581 (+35.8% year-to-date).

The Biggest Time Bomb — Fertilizer and Food

The Gulf accounts for 36% of the world's urea exports, 29% of ammonia, 26% of DAP, and about 50% of internationally traded sulfur. About a third of the world's fertilizer passes through Hormuz. Urea prices rose +50% by the end of March, and the World Bank forecasts a +31% rise in fertilizer and +60% in urea for 2026, assessing that affordability is at its worst since 2022.

The real risk that IFPRI points to is the secondary effect. As fertilizer prices rise and grain prices weaken, farmers cut back on fertilizer application, resulting in reduced harvests in 2026-27 and a food price shock arriving one season later. In other words, the food crisis is not behind us but ahead of us.

Rationing has already begun in various parts of the world — the Philippines declared its first national energy emergency on March 24, Slovenia converted to fuel rationing as the first in the EU (50 liters per person per day), Bangladesh implemented early university closures and 8 PM closing for commercial facilities, Myanmar enforced alternate-day operation, and Nepal cut LPG sales in half. According to Rystad's analysis, over 70% of the world's population lives in countries without IEA-standard (90-day) reserves.

Energy Transition

The Same Crisis Can Both Delay and Accelerate Transition

The supply shock created by the war acts on each country's clean energy transition in two directions. It may delay in the short term and accelerate in the medium to long term, and what determines the direction is not oil prices themselves but each country's energy, industrial, and import structure and policy will. (Korea Environment Institute KEI Focus No. 147, July 30, 2026)

Forces of Acceleration — Not the Logic of 'Climate' but 'Security'

  • Substitution effect. High oil prices increase the relative economic feasibility of renewable energy compared to fossil fuels, and also push forward electrification and efficiency through reduced vehicle use, expanded public transportation, and conversion to high-efficiency systems.
  • The key is not price but 'independence.' Imported fossil fuels are exposed to numerous variables such as war, sanctions, maritime transport, insurance, and chokepoint transit, while renewable energy requires no fuel imports and is structurally less vulnerable to supply disruptions and price volatility. Through the war, renewable energy is being reevaluated not just as a 'climate response tool' but as an economic and national security asset.
  • Major countries are already headed that way. The IEA viewed the crisis following the 2022 Ukraine war as potentially becoming a 'historic turning point' that accelerates clean energy investment, and the EU's Net-Zero Industry Act and Clean Industrial Deal, the US IRA, Japan's GX (Green Transformation) strategy, and China's mandatory renewable energy consumption and expanded facilities are all strengthening renewable energy through the logic of 'energy security + industrial competitiveness'. The OECD also recommended diversification of supply regions, energy sources, and technologies, along with efficiency improvements as a medium-term response to the 2026 crisis.

Forces of Delay — 'Energy Addition' and Return to Coal

  • In emergencies, there's a return to fossil fuels. When supply is short, governments prioritize short-term supply and price stability over decarbonization, increasing coal power operation rates, extending the lifespan of plants scheduled for closure, and restarting heavy oil power generation. Renewable energy also increases, but so does fossil fuel use, resulting in what's called 'energy addition.'
  • When LNG prices rise, there's a shift to coal. As natural gas, once used as a transition fuel, became more expensive, in the 2026 crisis the Philippines and Thailand reverted to gas-to-coal, while the Netherlands and Spain expanded renewables and electrification — responses to the same shock diverged in opposite directions.
  • Price subsidies erase the signal. When subsidies such as fuel tax cuts, price caps, and rate freezes are prolonged, the fossil fuel price felt by consumers falls below the actual social cost, working against transition. By April 2026, at least 26 OECD countries had introduced some form of energy price support, and if such temporary measures become entrenched, they could even hinder carbon pricing systems and energy tax reform.
What ultimately determines the direction

Leaning on short-term price subsidies and supply expansion delays the transition, while running efficiency, renewables, and electrification investment alongside institutional reforms in parallel speeds it up. External conditions such as international interest rates, clean energy supply chains, technology costs, and competition for resource security also shape each country's choices.

Korea — Energy Transition

Unifying Security and Climate — Diversification Alone Is Not Enough

This crisis has revealed an issue that goes beyond 'where to buy from' to 'what to depend on.' Korea carries a triple structure of Middle East-centered supply, fossil fuel-centered consumption, and high import dependence, so diversifying supplier countries alone can mitigate the risk but is unlikely to eliminate it. (Korea Environment Institute, KEI Focus No. 147, July 30, 2026)

Diversification Has Progressed, But Room for Maneuver Is Narrow

  • LNG has been significantly diversified. As of 2024, in addition to Qatar (19%) and Oman (10%), supply sources have expanded to include Australia, Malaysia, Indonesia, the United States, and others. However, 29% of the volume passes through Hormuz, directly exposing it to disruptions in strait passage.
  • Crude oil has little room for diversification. While supply lines are expanding to the Americas and Africa, Middle East dependence stood at 71.5% in 2024 (Korea Energy Economics Institute, 2025), leaving the structure still highly vulnerable to geopolitical shocks.
  • Reserves and diversification are short-term measures. Diversifying supplier countries and expanding reserves are important cards for mitigating short-term shocks, but they have limits in eliminating risk if geopolitical risk becomes medium- to long-term.

Core Strategy — A Transition That Reduces Demand Itself

  • Integrating security and climate. Expanding clean energy, efficiency, and electrification reduces greenhouse gases while also lowering dependence on imported fossil fuels, easing vulnerability to geopolitical crises and price volatility at the same time. For resource-poor Korea in particular, there is strong reason to treat the transition not merely as a 'carbon neutrality' task but as a top national strategy for energy security and independence.
  • Preventing carbon lock-in. Further expanding facilities and infrastructure based on imported fossil fuels now will cement dependence for decades to come. A foundation for decarbonization must be laid in advance through investment in clean energy, efficiency, and electrification, along with institutional improvements.

Response Tasks

  • National resilience. Continuously supplement emergency supply-demand plans in preparation for supply chain disruptions, and establish integrated public-private governance to forecast and monitor geopolitical risks.
  • Targeted support, minimized distortion. Expand support for vulnerable groups and industries during crises while reducing price distortions, designing measures that instead encourage conservation and efficiency.
  • Transition foundation. To ensure that the expansion of renewable energy translates into actual results, strengthen grid expansion, regulatory rationalization, energy storage system (ESS) deployment, and public acceptance together.
  • Consistency and acceptance. If the transition keeps wavering due to short-term oil prices and political conditions, it will breed policy distrust and dampen investment. Coherence among energy, climate, fiscal, and industrial policies must be strengthened, and social acceptance must be secured through support that eases the burden of transition.

Impasse

Eight Knots

  1. Hormuz Sovereignty — the deepest knot. Iranian Deputy Minister Gharibabadi: "The Hormuz was Iran's in the past, is Iran's now, and will be Iran's territory in the future." The U.S. position is that no single party should control the sea lane. For Iran, transit fees are both reconstruction funding and a permanent deterrent lever, which is exactly why neither the U.S. nor the Gulf can concede.
  2. U.S. Navy blockade. Iran wants this resolved first, while the U.S. sees it as the only lever remaining now that airstrikes have stopped. Which side unwinds it first is the real measure of victory or defeat.
  3. Reparations and the $300 billion fund. Iran demands reparations, while Trump instead demands that Iran pay reparations.   4. Scope and sequencing of sanctions. Iran insists on immediate, full lifting of the first and second rounds of sanctions, while the U.S. insists on phased easing tied to verified compliance. The revocation of the crude oil license on July 7 destroyed trust in the sequencing.
  5. Enrichment and verification. The U.S. demands "zero enrichment" and delivery of the 60% stockpile, while Iran asserts the rights of an NPT member state. The IAEA has been unable to gain access since February 28, leaving the situation too difficult to either draft or sell an agreement.
  6. Lebanon. The memorandum states "Lebanon included," but Israel is not a party. Until the October 27 general election, there is effectively no incentive for Israel to withdraw.
  7. Legitimacy of the mediator. Pakistan is distrusted by both Iran and Israel, Oman has earned Iran's trust but faces U.S. bombing threats, Qatar declines to take the lead, Turkey is blocked by its relationship with Israel, and the UN is paralyzed by the veto. Right now there is no stage acceptable to both sides.
  8. Domestic politics on both sides. Trump says he is "in no hurry" while demanding surrender, and Iran's negotiating faction cannot concede on the strait and enrichment after a war in which the Supreme Leader was killed. The U.S. midterm elections and Israel's general election dominate the timetable for any concessions.

Glossary and Who's Who

Things that often appear in articles but pass by without explanation.

Terms

Terms worth knowing to read the articles

Strait of Hormuz

The only exit connecting the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of Oman. Its narrowest point is about 33km, but the actual shipping lane width is only 3-9km. In normal times about 20 million barrels of crude oil per day (20-30% of the world's seaborne crude) and a significant share of the world's LNG pass through it, with 27% of Asia's LNG imports transiting here. Since Iran borders the entire northern coast, it can threaten passage with just fast boats, mines, and drones alone, and conversely Iran's own exports are also tied to the same channel, which has long been the conventional wisdom that "a full blockade is self-harm." 2026 is the year that conventional wisdom broke — Iran found a third way of selective passage and tolls instead of a full blockade.

Right of Transit Passage

A right stipulated in Part III of the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, guaranteeing continuous and expeditious passage of ships and aircraft through straits used for international navigation, even within the territorial waters of coastal states. It is a stronger right than innocent passage, and coastal states cannot impose permits or fees as conditions. This is the basis for claims that Iran's toll system is illegal, and the reason strait states such as Singapore strongly object (it would set a precedent for the Malacca, Gibraltar, and Danish straits).

Axis of Resistance

A network of armed groups sponsored by Iran. The term emerged as a response to Bush's 2002 'Axis of Evil,' and took concrete shape after the 2003 Iraq War. It includes Hezbollah (Lebanon), Hamas and Islamic Jihad (Palestine), the Houthis (Yemen), Shiite militias (Iraq), and, as a state, Syria. With Syria's collapse and Hezbollah's destruction in 2024, it has effectively entered a process of dissolution, which became a precondition for the 2026 invasion.

IRGC (Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps), Artesh, Basij, Quds Force

Iran has a dual military structure. The Artesh is the regular army protecting the state (about 350,000), while the IRGC is the revolutionary force protecting the regime (about 190,000), with its own separate army, navy, air force, and strategic missile force. The Quds Force is the IRGC's overseas operations arm (the organization Soleimani once led), and the Basij is a militia (90,000 standing, up to 1 million when mobilized) serving as the main force for suppressing protests. The IRGC holds not only military power but also vast economic interests spanning construction, energy, and telecommunications, making it effectively a state within a state. The August 2026 leadership reshuffle further entrenched the IRGC's political dominance.

Velayat-e Faqih (Guardianship of the Islamic Jurist) and the Assembly of Experts

A principle of Iran's theocratic system established by Khomeini, holding that the supreme Islamic jurist holds ultimate authority over the state. To this end, the Supreme Leader is in principle required to hold religious qualifications at the level of Ayatollah or Mujtahid. The election is carried out by the Assembly of Experts, composed of 88 clerics elected by the public. The core of the legitimacy dispute is that in March 2026, Mojtaba Khamenei was elected via an online vote at the rank of Hojatoleslam (one rank lower).

JCPOA and Snapback

The 2015 Iran nuclear deal. Sanctions were lifted in exchange for a 3.67% enrichment cap, a 300kg stockpile limit, 5,060 centrifuges, and a halt to enrichment at Fordow, among other things. It effectively collapsed after the US withdrawal in 2018, and snapback is the mechanism that automatically restores previously lifted UN sanctions in case of violations. The UK, France, and Germany triggered it on August 28, 2025, and sanctions were restored on September 27. Since the E3's authority to trigger it was itself set to expire on October 18, 2025, it amounted to using their last chance.

Enrichment levels — 3.67%, 20%, 60%, 90%

The U-235 ratio in natural uranium is 0.7%. Reactor fuel is 3-5%, research reactor fuel is 20%, and weapons-grade is 90% or higher. What matters is the nonlinearity of separative work units (SWU) — going from 0.7% to 20% requires 90% of the total work, while going from 60% to 90% takes only a small portion of the rest. In other words, a country with a 60% enriched stockpile has technically crossed nearly the entire threshold, which is why Iran's 440.9kg stockpile of 60% material is at the core of the negotiations.

Nuclear Hedging

A strategy of maintaining the capability and materials to build a nuclear weapon quickly if one decides to, without actually building one. It is also a concept applied to countries like Japan and South Korea. The IAEA and many experts assessed Iran's status as hedging, not weaponization, and this is at the core of the debate over the justification for going to war.

Bunker Buster (GBU-57 MOP) and B-2

An ultra-large bomb that penetrates deeply buried underground facilities. The GBU-57 weighs about 13.6 tons, and the only aircraft capable of carrying it is the U.S. Air Force's B-2 stealth bomber. Since Iran's nuclear facilities are located inside mountains, this is where the structure emerges that Israel alone is physically incapable of striking them, making direct U.S. involvement essential. In the June 2025 Operation Midnight Hammer, 7 B-2s dropped 14 bombs, and they were used again in 2026.

Decapitation Strike

An operation that paralyzes an organization by physically eliminating the enemy's leadership. The removal of Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei on February 28, 2026 is a representative example. Assessments of its effectiveness are divided — while the collapse of command and control contributed to a sharp drop in Iran's missile launch rate, the regime itself completed succession within ten days and did not collapse.

Force Majeure

A clause that declares that contract performance has become impossible due to war or disaster, exempting responsibility. When Qatar declared this on LNG supply in March 2026, four countries including South Korea had to immediately secure alternative volumes, and Shell and TotalEnergies declared chain force majeure to their own customers. Iraq, Kuwait, Bahrain, and the UAE also declared it on crude oil.

Dual Blockade

A state in which the U.S. Navy blockades Iranian ports while Iran blockades Hormuz, each entangling the other. It was established on April 13, lifted on May 29, and resumed on July 14. Because whichever side lifts it first loses leverage, the stalemate has dragged on.

PGSA (Persian Gulf Strait Authority)

A transit management body created by Iran on May 18, 2026. Shipowners must submit ownership, insurance, crew, cargo, and route information, and transit permission is granted only after approval and payment. The fee is $2 million per voyage or $1 per barrel, payable in yuan or cryptocurrency. The U.S. Treasury has warned that this payment could constitute a sanctions violation, placing shipping companies, charterers, and insurers in a legal trap.

Negative Lagging

A phenomenon in which, due to the time lag between when a refiner purchases crude oil and when it sells the product, inventory gains occur when oil prices rise, and inventory losses occur when they fall. This is why Korean refiners recorded record-high profits in the first half of 2026 but suffered a sharp margin deterioration in the second half.

SCFI · WCI · War Risk Premium

SCFI is the Shanghai Containerized Freight Index, and WCI is the Drewry World Container Index, both representative indicators of ocean freight rates. The War Risk Premium is assessed as a percentage of hull value, and jumped from a peacetime level of 0.125% to 7.5–10% in July 2026. This means that for a single voyage of a $100 million tanker, the insurance premium alone costs $7.5 million to $10 million.

The 12-Day War (June 2025)

A war that began with Israel's Operation Rising Lion on June 13, 2025 and ended in a ceasefire on June 24. Within hours of the outbreak, 30 Iranian generals and numerous nuclear scientists were eliminated, and on June 22 the United States joined with Operation Midnight Hammer, in which 7 B-2s dropped 14 GBU-57 bombs on Fordow and Natanz, and a submarine fired about 20 Tomahawk missiles at Isfahan. It was a rehearsal for and precedent of the 2026 war.

'Economic D-Day'

A sanctions campaign announced by President Trump on August 19, 2026. Targeting oil smuggling, swap lines, cash couriers, and exchange houses, he stated that there would be "tremendous consequences" for third countries as well. Coinciding on the same day with the UAE's complete halt of transactions with Iran, this symbolizes the stage of the war shifting from military to financial.
